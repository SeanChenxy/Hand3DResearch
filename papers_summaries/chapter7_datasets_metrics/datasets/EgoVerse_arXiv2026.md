# EgoVerse: An Egocentric Human Dataset for Robot Learning from Around the World

**Authors:** Ryan Punamiya, Simar Kareer, Zeyi Liu, Josh Citron, et al. (Georgia Tech; Stanford; UC San Diego; ETH Zurich; MIT; Meta Reality Labs; Mecka AI; Scale AI)

## Summary
Robot demonstration collection is expensive and hard to scale, while existing egocentric human datasets are static, one-off releases that lack manipulation-relevant annotations and systematic transfer evaluation. EgoVerse is a collaborative framework and dataset of 1,362 hours (80k episodes) of egocentric human demonstrations spanning 1,965 tasks, 240 scenes, and 2,087 demonstrators, contributed by a consortium of academic labs and industry partners and unified through EgoDB, a cloud-based living-dataset infrastructure. The dataset has two components: EgoVerse-A, controlled cross-lab data on six shared "Flagship Tasks" captured with Project Aria glasses, and EgoVerse-I, a nearly 1,400-hour industry-sourced stream of open-ended tasks with dense language annotations. The paper also reports the most comprehensive human-to-robot transfer study to date, replicated across three robot embodiments at multiple labs, finding that co-training with human data improves performance (up to 30% in-domain and out-of-domain), that scaling benefits require domain-aligned human–robot data as an anchor, and that scene diversity contributes more to generalization than demonstrator diversity under limited budgets.

## Background and Motivation
Existing human-activity datasets (Ego4D, EgoExo4D, Something-Something V2, EPIC-Kitchens, HOI4D) are not designed for robot learning: they include tasks beyond robot capability, lack hand-pose and interaction annotations, and contain unstructured activities. Newer manipulation-relevant datasets and prior human-to-robot co-training work (EgoMimic, EgoDex, EgoBridge, Humanoid Policy ~ Human Policy) demonstrated co-training benefits but at limited scale, with a single robot embodiment, or as static releases. EgoVerse's premise is "bounded diversity" — tasks feasible for typical bimanual mobile manipulators, but with natural variation across environments, objects, and demonstrators — plus a continuously growing ecosystem rather than a fixed dataset, and a reproducible, cross-lab, cross-embodiment evaluation to identify which transfer findings are robust.

## Dataset Construction
- **Capture hardware:** EgoVerse-A academic partners use Project Aria Gen 1 glasses (75 g head-worn device; wide-FoV RGB camera plus two synchronized monochrome scene cameras for SLAM and hand tracking that keep hands visible outside the forward view). EgoVerse-I comes from industry partners' custom wearable rigs, typically head-mounted stereo fisheye RGB cameras for accurate hand-pose estimation, with depth streams where available and inertial sensing for camera motion. A phone-based system (iPhone on a head strap, ultrawide camera at 1080p/30 FPS) with a cloud pipeline that recovers 6-DoF head pose and 21-keypoint 3D hand poses opens capture to the broader community.
- **Scale:** 1,362 hours, 80k episodes, 1,965 tasks, 240 scenes, 2,087 demonstrators in the current release.
- **EgoVerse-A:** standardized collection across participating labs organized around ~5-minute dataset units yielding 5–10 demonstrations per task, with quality constraints on hand/object visibility and logged metadata. Six Flagship Tasks shared across all labs: object-in-container (single-arm, 40-second repeated pick/place/dump), cup-on-saucer (bimanual reorientation and placement), bag-grocery (bimanual, open bag and load 1–3 items), fold-clothes (bimanual three-fold of a T-shirt), scoop-granular (single-arm transfer of granular material), sort-utensils (single-arm sorting). Each flagship task is performed across 8–12 scenes per lab with 1–10 units per scene, within a roughly 40cm x 60cm workspace with randomized object placement, up to 30 objects per task per lab (independent procurement across sites introduces object variation), and 1–8 demonstrators per lab. A Controlled-Diversity Subset from one lab uses a fixed pool of 16 demonstrators and 16 scenes with a structured assignment matrix (3.75 minutes to 2 hours per demonstrator–scene pair; at least 1 hour per scene) to independently vary demonstrator and scene diversity.
- **EgoVerse-I:** nearly 1,400 hours across nearly 2,000 tasks (over 1,500 open-ended tasks spanning everyday activity categories), 240 scenes, 2,087 demonstrators, collected in the wild by industry partners with manual quality control retaining manipulation-dense segments and instructions to keep hands visible. UMAP of DINOv3 embeddings on fold-clothes shows EgoVerse-I substantially expands visual coverage beyond EgoVerse-A and lab robot data.
- **Annotations:** per-frame 3D hand pose (21 keypoints per hand) and calibrated 6-DoF head pose from visual–inertial SLAM (Project Aria Machine Perception Service for academic data; partner SLAM, model-based pose estimation, and post-processing for industry data). EgoVerse-A carries per-episode task descriptions, scene identifiers, primary manipulated objects, and demonstrator metadata; EgoVerse-I adds denser annotations including fine-grained (1–2 s) language descriptions, active-hand indicators, and static-versus-mobile manipulation flags.

## Evaluation Protocol
EgoDB (S3-backed storage, nightly processing/validation/indexing, SQL metadata database, web viewer, configuration-driven subset sync) manages the data. The transfer study co-trains robot policies with human data using a cross-embodiment BC co-training architecture: modality-specific stems (ResNet-18 for egocentric RGB shared across human and robot, separate wrist-camera stems, MLP proprioception encoders), a shared transformer encoder with learnable tokens, and flow-matching action decoders (shared or embodiment-specific). Human actions are camera-centered projected SE(3) hand-pose trajectories; robot actions are normalized (quantile normalization to [-1,1]) 6-DoF end-effector poses with gripper state. Three robot platforms: Robot A (two upright 6-DoF ARX5 arms with parallel-jaw grippers, Aria glasses ego camera, two wrist RealSense D405s), Robot B (ARX5 arms side-mounted on a human-like 3D-printed shoulder rig, Aria glasses, wrist Logitech webcams), and Robot C (Unitree G1 humanoid with 7-DoF arms and 6-DoF Inspire dexterous hands, ZED 2 stereo ego camera). Evaluation uses four representative Flagship tasks with 20 in-domain and 20 out-of-domain rollouts per task (randomized initial conditions; OOD uses unseen objects/environments), scored by task-specific subtask metrics (grasps, placements, intermediate manipulations, full completion) aggregated into a normalized score; robot post-training uses approximately 150–300 teleoperated demonstrations per task. Scaling and diversity studies additionally use an offline Avg-MSE metric on held-out human data.

## Findings and Analysis
- Co-training with EgoVerse-A human data consistently improves in-domain and OOD robot performance by up to 30% relative across the three robots; one exception is a performance decrease on bag-grocery for Robot B, attributed to that embodiment forcing robot demonstrations to deviate from human strategies.
- Scaling with diverse human data works only when domain-aligned (task-matched) human data anchors training: neither 8 hours of diverse EgoVerse-A data nor domain-aligned human data alone yields significant gains, but adding as little as 2 hours of aligned data enables transfer from 2 hours of diverse data, with gains growing as diverse data scales to 8 hours.
- Demonstrator-diversity scaling (1, 2, 4, 8, 16 demonstrators in a single scene at a fixed 2-hour budget; 4, 8, 12 demonstrators across 8 scenes at a fixed 8-hour budget) consistently improves generalization to unseen demonstrators on fold-clothes, measured by offline Avg-MSE (e.g., single-scene Avg-MSE decreasing from about 0.075–0.08 at 1 demonstrator to about 0.055 at 16).
- Scene-diversity scaling (1, 2, 4, 8, 16 scenes at fixed demonstrator pool) improves generalization to unseen scenes across data budgets; once data quantity is moderate, increasing data density within scenes yields diminishing returns while expanding scene coverage keeps improving — scene diversity dominates under limited data budgets. Joint scaling (4–8 scenes, 4 vs 8 demonstrators, fixed 4-hour budget) shows complementary improvements with decreasing marginal benefit of extra demonstrators as scene coverage grows.

## Contributions
1. A 1,362-hour / 80k-episode egocentric human dataset (1,965 tasks, 240 scenes, 2,087 demonstrators) with standardized formats and manipulation-relevant annotations (3D hand poses, head poses, subtask-level descriptions).
2. EgoVerse-A, a protocol-controlled cross-lab benchmark component with six shared Flagship Tasks and a Controlled-Diversity Subset that decouples scene and demonstrator diversity.
3. EgoVerse-I, the largest action-labeled egocentric human dataset claimed at the time (nearly 1,400 hours, nearly 2,000 tasks) from industry in-the-wild capture with dense 1–2 s language annotations.
4. EgoDB, a living-dataset infrastructure for continuous multi-source ingestion, processing, and access, plus a phone-based community capture pipeline.
5. A consortium-scale, cross-embodiment study establishing that co-training with human data helps, that aligned human–robot data is the gating factor for scaling, and that scene diversity matters more than demonstrator diversity for environment generalization.

## Limitations
The paper's own Limitations section states that the study focuses on human-and-robot co-training, leaving broader algorithmic exploration (e.g., pre-training and fine-tuning recipes) to future work. The scene- and demonstrator-diversity experiments rely exclusively on offline Avg-MSE metrics on human data; the authors note that robot rollouts are needed to confirm that these diversity effects translate into improved robotic generalization. Evaluation of the flagship tasks is confined to bimanual/single-arm tabletop-style tasks feasible for the three tested embodiments, and the transfer findings, while replicated, depend on embodiment-specific action spaces and camera configurations.
