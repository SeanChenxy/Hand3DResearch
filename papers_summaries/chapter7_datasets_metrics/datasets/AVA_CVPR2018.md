# AVA: A Video Dataset of Spatio-temporally Localized Atomic Visual Actions

**Authors:** Chunhui Gu, Chen Sun, David A. Ross, Carl Vondrick  
**Date:** 2018 (CVPR 2018)  
**Identifier:** [arXiv:1705.08421](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08421)  
**Zotero item:** No record found in the Zotero library; identity verified against arXiv metadata.  
**Evidence status:** No Zotero record; verified against full-text PDF extraction (arXiv 1705.08421).

## Summary
The paper introduces AVA, a video dataset that densely annotates 80 atomic visual actions in 430 fifteen-minute movie clips, with actions localized both spatially (per-person bounding boxes) and temporally (1 Hz keyframe sampling with plus/minus 1.5 seconds of context), producing 1.58M action labels with multiple labels per person occurring frequently. Its five defining characteristics are: atomic rather than composite action classes, precise spatio-temporal annotation with multiple labels per person, exhaustive labeling of all people in each clip, temporally linked persons across consecutive segments, and movie sourcing for varied action representation. The authors also propose a state-of-the-art spatio-temporal action localization method combining Faster R-CNN region proposals with I3D tube classification, which sets new marks on JHMDB and UCF101-24 yet reaches only 15.6% frame-mAP on AVA, exposing the difficulty of atomic action recognition that clip-level datasets had hidden.

## Background and Motivation
Popular action datasets either provide trimmed clips for whole-clip classification (KTH, HMDB, UCF101, and large-scale sets such as Sports-1M, YouTube-8M, Kinetics), temporal but not spatial localization (ActivityNet, THUMOS, MultiTHUMOS, Charades), or spatial annotations only for short clips with few composite actions (JHMDB, UCF Sports). Real-world applications instead require continuous annotation of atomic actions of all humans, which can be composed into higher-level events. Drawing on Barker and Wright's observation that activity is hierarchical, the authors argue that at fine time scales actions are physical with clear visual signatures, making a 1 Hz sampling sufficient to capture semantic content while avoiding the inherently fuzzy action boundaries noted by THUMOS. Exhaustive labeling also avoids the retrieval bias of keyword-based web collection: an "opening a door" clip tagged on YouTube is attention-worthy and atypical, whereas exhaustively labeled movie clips follow a realistic Zipf's-law distribution.

## Dataset Construction
- **Source and scale:** Movies from YouTube, restricted to videos with "film" or "television" topic annotation, over 30 minutes long, uploaded at least 1 year prior, with at least 1000 views; black-and-white, low-resolution, animated, cartoon, gaming, and mature content excluded. Each movie contributes only its 15th-to-30th minute (skipping titles and trailers), giving 430 fifteen-minute clips; at 1 Hz sampling this yields nearly 900 keyframes per movie, and each clip is partitioned into 897 overlapping 3-second segments with a stride of 1 second.
- **Vocabulary:** 80 atomic visual actions following three principles—generality (daily-life actions, not environment-specific), atomicity (clear visual signatures, object-independent, e.g., "hold" without specifying the object), and exhaustivity (iterated until covering about 99% of actions annotators labeled). The vocabulary contains 14 pose classes, 49 person-object interaction classes, and 17 person-person interaction classes.
- **Bounding boxes:** A hybrid scheme uses a high-precision Faster R-CNN person detector for initial boxes, with annotators adding missed persons; manual annotation retrieves only 5% more boxes, and incorrect boxes are flagged and removed.
- **Person linking:** Bounding boxes in adjacent keyframes are matched with a person embedding and the Hungarian algorithm; humans verify each match, yielding 81,000 tracklets ranging from seconds to minutes.
- **Action annotation:** Two-stage propose-then-verify crowdsourcing (motivated by recall loss over 80 classes); up to 7 labels per person per keyframe—1 required pose, up to 3 optional person-object, up to 3 optional person-person interactions—plus an "other action" flag. Each segment takes 22 seconds to propose and 19.7 seconds to verify; each clip is annotated by 3 independent annotators and a label counts as ground truth only if verified by at least 2.
- **Statistics:** Multiple labels per box are common: every box has one pose label, 28% have at least one person-object interaction, and 67% have at least one person-person interaction. The label distribution roughly follows Zipf's law ("stand" 208,332 trainval instances versus 141 for "swim"). Videos are split at the video level into 235 training, 64 validation, and 131 test videos (roughly 55:15:30), yielding 211k training, 57k validation, and 118k test segments.

## Evaluation Protocol
The task is spatio-temporal action detection: given a keyframe and its temporal context, localize all persons with bounding boxes and classify their atomic actions (non-mutually-exclusive labels). The benchmark uses the 60 classes with at least 25 instances in both validation and test splits—210,634 training, 57,371 validation, and 117,441 test examples—reporting frame-level mAP at IoU 0.5 (PASCAL VOC protocol) and video-level mAP using 3D IoU between linked tubes at thresholds 0.5 and 0.2. The authors' baseline model generates region proposals with a 2D ResNet-50 Faster R-CNN on the RGB keyframe, extracts I3D feature maps at the Mixed 4e layer from RGB and FlowNet-v2 optical flow streams, applies 3D ROI pooling by replicating 2D ROI pooling across time, and fuses the streams at the conv4 level; the softmax loss is replaced by per-class sigmoid losses to handle multi-labels, and frame detections are linked into tubes with dynamic-programming linking. The model is initialized from ImageNet (ResNet) and Kinetics-pretrained (I3D) weights.

## Findings and Analysis
- **AVA results:** the full model reaches 15.6% frame-mAP@0.5 and 12.3% video-mAP@0.5 (17.9% video-mAP@0.2) on the validation set, and 14.7% frame-mAP@0.5 on the test set.
- **State of the art elsewhere:** the same 3D two-stream model obtains 73.3% frame-mAP and 78.6% video-mAP@0.5 on JHMDB (versus 65.7% and 73.7% for ACT), and 76.3% frame-mAP and 59.9% video-mAP@0.5 on UCF101-24 (versus 69.5% and 51.4% for ACT).
- **Temporal context matters more on AVA:** frame-mAP improves from 13.6% (5 RGB + 5 flow) to 15.6% (40 RGB + 40 flow) on AVA, while JHMDB and UCF101-24 saturate around 20 frames (76.4% and 78.3%); the RGB-only variant reaches 14.5% and flow-only 9.9% on AVA.
- **Localization versus recognition:** class-agnostic actor detection reaches 75.3% frame-mAP on AVA versus 15.6% for full action detection—a gap of nearly 60%, versus under 15% on JHMDB and UCF101-24—so AVA's difficulty lies in action classification, not localization, and stems from fine-grained distinctions (high-scoring false alarms for "smoking", confusion of "touch" and "hold").
- **Class effects:** scene-correlated or low-diversity classes ("swim", "fall down") score high despite few training examples, while fine-grained interaction classes with many examples remain hard; more data generally helps but classes are not equally complex.

## Contributions
- The AVA dataset: 1.58M spatio-temporally localized action labels for 80 atomic actions over 430 dense 15-minute movie clips, with per-person boxes, person tracklets (81,000), and multi-label annotation, released publicly at https://research.google.com/ava/.
- An exhaustively annotated, person-centric 1 Hz benchmark with temporally linked subjects that supports both action detection and temporal modeling of action sequences (e.g., "look at phone" to "answer phone"; "ride" and "drive" co-occurring across persons).
- A state-of-the-art spatio-temporal localization baseline (Faster R-CNN proposals plus I3D tube classification) that demonstrates the gap between current capability and AVA's difficulty.
- Quantitative evidence that prior benchmark performance overstated progress: realistic scene and action complexity drives frame-mAP down to 15.6% from over 73% on established datasets.

## Limitations
- Movie sourcing introduces bias: film is shaped by narrative conventions and a "grammar of the film language", and the depiction of action is biased (e.g., by gender, citing the Geena Davis Institute report), so AVA does not reflect the true distribution of human activity.
- The action-label distribution follows Zipf's law; the benchmark excludes rare classes (fewer than 25 val/test instances), and long-tail classes are sensitive to training-set size.
- Two-stage annotation is needed because annotators inevitably miss correct actions from an 80-class vocabulary; recall for small classes depends heavily on the verification stage.
- Temporal localization precision is bounded by the 1 Hz keyframe design (about plus or minus 0.5 seconds) rather than precise boundary annotation.
- Composite activities (e.g., "eating in a restaurant") are out of scope and left as future work to be modeled from the atomic actions.
