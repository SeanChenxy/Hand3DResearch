# Human Universal Grasping

**Authors:** Kevin Yuanbo Wu, Tianxing Zhou, Isaac Tu, Billy Yan
**Date:** 2026 (arXiv)
**Identifier:** [arXiv:2606.17054](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.17054)

## Summary
Multi-fingered robot grasping lacks the scale and diversity of real interaction data that humans accumulate naturally. This paper argues for human grasps as the data source and contributes three artifacts: 1M-HUG_S, an egocentric dataset of 1M image-grasp pairs collected with Aria Gen 2 smart glasses across 41 buildings; HUG, a flow-matching model that predicts diverse MANO grasps from a single RGB-D image and a user click, retargetable to multiple robot hands without per-embodiment training; and HUG-Bench, a benchmark of 90 unseen objects in five geometric categories with metric-scale simulation meshes for paired simulation and real-robot evaluation. HUG reaches 73.0% simulation success on the benchmark's test split and 66.7% real-world tabletop success, beating state-of-the-art baselines by +23% and +34%, and 62.0% in-the-wild on a new robot embodiment in an unseen home.

## Background and Motivation
Existing dexterous grasp data comes from two paradigms with complementary weaknesses: synthetic grasp synthesis (DexGraspNet, Dex1B) scales broadly but suffers sim-to-real gaps and typically needs complete object point clouds; teleoperation yields real grasps but is tedious and embodiment-bound. In-the-wild human datasets either lack precise 3D signals or are confined to lab settings. Two recent advances enable a third path: lightweight egocentric smart glasses that stream calibrated RGB-D and 3D hand tracking make human grasp collection as easy as wearing glasses, and learned human-to-robot retargeting narrows the morphology gap for anthropomorphic hands. The paper builds a collect-learn-retarget pipeline on these advances, using no robot data at all.

## Dataset Construction
1M-HUG_S is recorded with Aria Gen 2 glasses, which provide synchronized RGB and stereo grayscale views, 6-DoF camera poses, and 21 3D hand landmarks. In each of 6,707 recordings (27.8 hours total), the wearer stands before a target object, moves their head for 15-30 seconds capturing the scene without hands visible, then grasps it with the right hand; the single physical grasp is back-propagated into all preceding no-hand frames using the camera poses, so one recording yields hundreds of (object-only image, grasp) training pairs from diverse viewpoints at no annotation cost. Curation uses a vision-language model to identify the grasped object, SAM3 to propagate its mask, stability and proximity heuristics to select the grasp frame, and human review of every recording; five frame filters require a non-empty mask, at least 60% confident stereo depth (via S2M2), hand-landmark/object-mask intersection, at least five in-bounds landmarks, and absence of the hand. After filtering, the dataset retains 1M RGB plus 1M grayscale frames (2M training entries) covering an estimated ~1.5K unique objects across 41 buildings and hundreds of environments. Each entry contains a 224x224 image, camera intrinsics, a metric depth map, an object mask, and the grasp pose in the camera frame; the 21 landmarks are fitted to a full articulated MANO hand with shape fixed at one canonical value so identical pose parameters denote the same grasp across collectors, and an anatomical-validity prior regularizes poses. The full pipeline is released as aria2mano. HUG-Bench, the evaluation benchmark, comprises 90 objects (unseen in training) spanning five geometric categories (cylindrical, spheroidal, prismatic, appendaged, amorphous) and three size bins, six objects per class combination (four validation, two test); the 30 test objects cost about 250 USD and are publicly purchasable. Metric-scale simulation meshes are produced by extending MV-SAM3D with Aria intrinsics, extrinsics, and stereo depth over 18 five-object scan scenes, aligned to the SLAM point cloud, made watertight with Alpha Wrap, and decomposed with CoACD; 10 human grasps per object are recorded as a replayable oracle. The scan-to-asset pipeline and MuJoCo environment are released as aria2mesh. Project website: https://grasping.io.

## Evaluation Protocol
Grasp generation: given an RGB-D image and a 2D click, HUG predicts a 99-dim grasp state (wrist translation, 6D wrist rotation, and 15 MANO finger-joint rotations) via a flow-matching transformer over DINOv2 RGB tokens fused with PointNeXt point-cloud tokens by point painting. Simulation evaluation (MuJoCo): a position-actuated MANO right hand executes an open-loop pre-grasp, grasp, lift rollout on a single object resting under gravity on a table; success means the object leaves the surface at the end of the lift. Metrics are success rate (SR) and fingertip contact (FC) error, the average of thumb and closest-finger signed distances to the object surface, averaged over 10 grasps per object on 60 validation and 30 test objects; checkpoints are selected by best validation SR. Baselines in simulation include RGB-only and PC-only ablations and the human grasp oracle. Real-world evaluation: 10 trials per test object (300 per method) on the tabletop setup (ZED stereo camera, xArm, 6-DoF Ability hand) for HUG and Dex1B, with CAP in its published parallel-jaw configuration; an in-the-wild study transfers HUG zero-shot to a YOR mobile manipulator with an AgileX NERO arm, 20-DoF WUJI hand, and Aria Gen 2 vision across rooms of an unseen home.

## Findings and Analysis
On HUG-Bench simulation, the full model achieves 71.5% val and 73.0% test SR with FC error 19.0 mm and 14.6 mm, within roughly 20 points of the replayed human oracle (90.3% val, 94.0% test SR, FC 9.4/7.4 mm). The auxiliary 3D landmark loss is the most critical component: removing it drops test SR by over 40 points to 32.7% and doubles FC error to 35.7 mm; removing the 0.3 m point-cloud crop or DINO point painting each costs about 10 val-SR and 15 test-SR points. PC-only remains strong (70.7% test SR, FC 22.1 mm) while RGB-only collapses (29.7% test SR, FC 108.6 mm), showing complementary roles: depth reaches the object, RGB provides semantic grounding of grasp sites. Scaling training data from 25K to 1M frames raises test SR from 33% to 73% and reduces FC error from 54.2 mm to 14.6 mm, without saturation. In the real world on the 30 test objects, HUG achieves 66.7% tabletop success versus 43.7% for Dex1B and 32.7% for CAP, succeeding on 28/30 objects at least once, with the largest margins on large prismatic objects (storage bin 10/10 versus 0/10 for both baselines) and handled or irregular objects (picnic basket 9/10 versus 1/10 and 0/10). Zero-shot in-the-wild deployment on a different robot hand and camera reaches 62.0% (29/30 objects with at least one success), only 4.7 points below tabletop. Failure analysis attributes most failures to object or surface contact during closing (42 and 57 hit-object cases in the two settings) and to slippage during lift, with the football and wipe dispenser ungraspable by the small Ability hand.

## Contributions
- 1M-HUG_S, the first million-scale egocentric dataset of natural human grasps, with MANO-fit grasp labels and metric depth from smart glasses across 41 buildings, released with its full aria2mano pipeline.
- HUG, a flow-matching grasp generator from a single RGB-D image and click that retargets zero-shot to multiple robot embodiments, trained entirely on human data.
- HUG-Bench, a benchmark of 90 difficult unseen objects with metric-scale meshes, paired simulation (MuJoCo) and real-robot protocols, recorded human-grasp oracles, and a purchasable 30-object test set for unbiased evaluation.
- Demonstration of cross-embodiment, cross-camera, and cross-environment generalization with detailed scaling and failure-mode analyses.

## Limitations
Training covers right-hand grasps only, with MANO shape fixed at a canonical value, so left-handed, bimanual, and hand-size-specific morphology are not modeled. Retargeting can fail when a robot hand cannot realize the predicted MANO pose, and open-loop execution lacks visual or force feedback, causing pre-grasp collisions and post-grasp slips. Grasp labels degrade under hand occlusion due to Aria hand-tracking errors. Accuracy drops on very small objects (limited by 224x224 inputs) and on large or distant objects that are rare in egocentric data. Only one grasp is predicted and executed per trial, and evaluation is restricted to indoor scenes.
