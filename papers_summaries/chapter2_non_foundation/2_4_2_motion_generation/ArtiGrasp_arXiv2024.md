# ArtiGrasp: Physically Plausible Synthesis of Bi-Manual Dexterous Grasping and Articulation

**Authors:** Hui Zhang, Sammy Christen, Zicong Fan, Luocheng Zheng, Jemin Hwangbo, Jie Song, Otmar Hilliges  
**Date:** 2024-03-03  
**Identifier:** [arXiv:2309.03891](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.03891)

## Summary

ArtiGrasp trains reinforcement-learning (RL) policies in physics simulation to synthesize bi-manual hand-object interactions that combine grasping, relocation, and articulation of articulated objects from only a single static hand pose reference per interaction. A two-phase curriculum (fixed-base objects with separate per-hand environments, then non-fixed objects with both hands sharing one environment) enables the precise finger control needed for articulation. On the newly introduced Dynamic Object Grasping and Articulation task, ArtiGrasp reaches a 0.50 task success rate versus 0.11 for an adapted D-Grasp baseline — a 5x improvement — and remains robust when driven by noisy hand-object poses regressed from single RGB images.

## Background and Problem

Realistic hand-object interaction generation matters for animation, AR/VR, human-computer interaction, and robotics, and could reduce the cost of marker-based motion capture for two-handed manipulation such as operating a coffee machine or opening a laptop. Existing synthesis methods split into data-driven approaches (e.g., CAMS for articulated objects, ManipNet for two-hand manipulation) that require full per-frame 3D motion supervision, and physics-based RL approaches (e.g., D-Grasp) that need only a single hand pose reference but were restricted to single-hand grasping of rigid objects. The paper identifies a gap: no prior framework synthesizes bi-manual grasping and articulation in one physically plausible pipeline. Two core challenges are that articulation demands diverse wrist motions (a simple PD-control scheme for wrist relocation fails here) and that successful articulation requires precise finger control, where small deviations from ideal contact points hurt performance and one hand can obstruct the other. The proposed task, Dynamic Object Grasping and Articulation, takes an articulated object (two parts rotating about a joint axis) with an initial 7-DoF pose (6-DoF base pose plus 1-DoF joint angle) and a target articulated pose, plus two pairs of object-relative hand pose references (one for grasping, one for articulation), and requires generating hand trajectories that bring the object from the initial to the target state through grasping, relocation, and articulation.

## Method

ArtiGrasp trains one RL policy per hand, formulated as an MDP with PPO, in the RaiSim physics simulator, using controllable MANO hand models with 51 DoFs each (45 local joint rotations plus a 6-DoF global pose) and object meshes from ARCTIC split into a base part and an articulation part connected by a single joint. Policies output PD-control targets from which joint torques are computed. A feature extraction layer maps the simulation state and the hand pose reference into hand features (joint rotations, velocities, per-link net contact forces, and hand velocities in the object-relative frame), object features (the 7-DoF articulated pose and velocity in the wrist-relative frame, plus articulation-specific features: the articulation-axis direction, wrist-to-axis direction, wrist-to-axis distance, and masses of the two parts), and goal features (differences between target and current joint angles and positions, target versus current binary contact vectors, and the difference between target and current articulation angle). The reward combines an imitation term (joint position and angle errors against the reference, a contact reward covering the fraction of achieved target contacts and the contact force capped proportionally to object weight, and velocity regularization of hand and object) and a task term (driving the joint angle to the target while keeping the object base near its initial position). A two-phase curriculum addresses the precision required for articulation: in phase one the object base is fixed to the table and each hand trains in its own environment, letting policies learn fine finger control quickly (simulation speed scales roughly quadratically with the number of contacts); in phase two the object is non-fixed and both hands share one physics environment, forcing cooperation — one hand must grasp stably so the other can articulate without tipping the object. At inference, the Dynamic Object Grasping and Articulation task is composed by chaining phases: grasp with the articulation angle set to zero, relocate via D-Grasp's motion synthesis module, transition from grasp to pre-grasp poses using a heuristics-based scheme (opening the fingers, moving away from the object along the wrist-to-center direction, and linearly interpolating to pre-grasp poses), and finally run the articulation policy to reach the target angle.

## Contributions

1. A physically plausible RL-based method for the Dynamic Object Grasping and Articulation task, unifying grasping and articulation in a single policy per hand guided by a single hand pose reference, without object- or task-specific retraining.
2. A general reward function and training scheme that learns fine-grained wrist and finger control for grasping and articulating a diverse set of articulated objects.
3. A two-phase learning curriculum of increasing difficulty (fixed-base, separate-hand environments followed by non-fixed, cooperative two-hand training) that makes learning precise articulation feasible.
4. Demonstration that the pipeline works not only with motion-capture references but also with noisy hand-object pose estimates from an off-the-shelf image-based regressor, enabling motion synthesis from single images.

Compared to prior work (Table 1 of the paper), ArtiGrasp is the only method listed that simultaneously uses physics simulation, handles two-hand free-base manipulation, and requires only few-shot static hand pose references while accommodating both rigid and articulated objects.

## Experimental Setup

Training uses PPO with RaiSim on a single Nvidia RTX 6000 GPU and 128 CPU cores, taking roughly three days; supplementary details include 1e4 epochs, 6e5 steps per epoch, 300 environment steps per episode, a 2.5e-3 s simulation timestep with 4 steps per action, and a 2-layer, 128-unit policy network with Adam (learning rate 5e-4). The data comes from the ARCTIC dataset of annotated bimanual interaction with articulated objects; the authors exclude the objects "scissors" (no clean base/articulation split), "phone," and "capsule machine" (thin articulation parts), extract one pair of hand pose references per interaction via heuristics, and create a 65%/35% train/test split over training and validation sequences, yielding 488 training and 257 test hand pose references for 8 objects (745 total). Evaluation metrics follow prior work: grasp success (object lifted above 0.1 m without falling), position error (m), angle error (rad, geodesic), articulation success (joint moved more than 0.3 rad without slipping), articulation angle error (rad), simulated distance (base displacement in m), and task success (PE < 0.05 m, AE < 0.2 rad, AAE < 0.5 rad). Baselines are vanilla D-Grasp with both hand policies trained on non-stationary objects, and a PD+IK controller that tracks the reference poses, with D-Grasp's wrist control adapted for articulation by incrementing the joint angle and solving inverse kinematics assuming the wrist is rigidly attached to the articulating part. Grasping is evaluated on 30 randomly sampled 6-DoF target object poses per reference; articulation on 5 target angles (0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.25, 1.5 rad); the composed task combines all grasp and articulation references per object with randomly sampled target articulated poses, producing roughly 7500 evaluation trials.

## Results

On the decoupled tasks, ArtiGrasp matches D-Grasp on grasping (success 0.71 vs 0.72; PD+IK 0.13) and substantially outperforms both baselines on articulation (success 0.55 vs 0.22 for D-Grasp and 0.28 for PD+IK; articulation angle error 0.57 vs 0.93 and 0.80 rad; simulated base displacement 0.01 vs 0.49 and 0.39 m), showing the learned wrist control is essential for articulation. On the composed Dynamic Object Grasping and Articulation task, ArtiGrasp achieves a task success rate of 0.50 versus 0.11 for adapted D-Grasp (a 5x gain), with better position error (0.03 vs 0.05 m), angle error (0.10 vs 0.15 rad), and articulation angle error (0.41 vs 0.66 rad), indicating D-Grasp struggles when grasping and articulation must be composed. With hand pose references reconstructed from single RGB images of the unseen validation subject s05 (60 references) using ARCTIC's image-based regressor, ArtiGrasp retains comparable performance (grasp success 0.64 vs 0.67 with mocap; articulation success 0.54 in both cases), demonstrating robustness to reconstruction noise such as hand-object interpenetration. Ablations confirm each component matters: removing the curriculum drops articulation success from 0.55 to 0.36 (grasping slightly improves to 0.74), training without cooperation drops grasp success to 0.21, and removing the articulation features reduces articulation success to 0.48 while degrading all articulation metrics. Qualitatively, the policies show recovery behaviors, such as retrying a failed grasp until articulation succeeds, and can compose long multi-object sequences (mixer, box, ketchup bottle) with a heuristics-based planner. A proof-of-concept with a single left-out object shows generalization to unseen objects with about a 15% performance drop, which the authors attribute to the hand pose reference acting as a strong prior.

## Limitations

The method sometimes produces unnatural hand poses, caused by noisy hand pose references (the index finger is often poorly labeled in ARCTIC) and by the trade-off between task and imitation rewards; the authors suggest integrating biomechanical constraints or learned hand pose priors as a remedy. Transitions between grasping, relocation, and articulation phases, as well as multi-object sequence planning, rely on heuristic control schemes rather than a learned high-level planner. Objects that cannot be split into a clear base plus articulation part (scissors) or have small, thin articulation parts (phone, capsule machine) are outside the current model's scope. Generalization is only demonstrated in a proof-of-concept on a single left-out object with a roughly 15% performance drop, pending larger 3D datasets of articulated objects. Evaluation also omits an interpenetration metric because all compared methods run inside physics simulation, and reference-free generation (without any hand pose reference) remains future work.
