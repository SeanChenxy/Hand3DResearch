## Summary

D-Grasp introduces the dynamic grasp synthesis task: given an object with a known 6D pose and a single static grasp reference, generate physically plausible hand motions that grasp the object and move it to a target 6D pose. The method combines a reinforcement-learning low-level grasping policy with a high-level motion synthesis module inside a physics simulation, using only one grasp label per sequence instead of full demonstrations. In simulation it lifts grasp success rates far above directly replaying the references (e.g., 0.70 versus 0.31 for PD-controlled DexYCB ground truth on the training set, while keeping hand-object interpenetration low), and a hierarchical decomposition with an actively controlled grasp is decisive for moving objects without dropping them.

## Background and Problem

Hands are the primary means of human manipulation, and while hand-object pose estimation and static grasp synthesis have been studied intensively, human grasping is a continuous interaction: the hand must maintain a stable grasp under friction, contact, and object dynamics while moving the object. Prior data-driven methods generate only static grasps, and learning-based dexterous-hand control typically relies on anchored hands or costly full demonstration trajectories. The paper defines the new task of dynamic grasp synthesis: given a grasp label D = (qh, Th, To) — the hand's 6D global pose and local finger pose at a static grasp instance together with the object pose — synthesize a trajectory that approaches the object, establishes a stable grasp, and moves the object into a user-specified 6D goal pose Tg. Key challenges are reasoning about continuous hand-object contact (a small hand-pose error can make the object slip), the fact that contact is typically unobservable in images and grasp stability is hard to assess statically, and the need to generate smooth, human-like trajectories without dense supervision. Downstream applications include AR/VR, robotics, and HCI. To the authors' knowledge this is the first method studying this task.

## Method

D-Grasp formulates the task as a Markov Decision Process solved with PPO in the RaiSim physics simulator. The hand is a controllable model built from the MANO skeleton (mean shape) with 45 finger-joint actuators plus 6 DoF for the global pose, driven by PD controllers; the policy outputs residual actions added to a bias term (the current joint configuration for fingers), which yields smoother motion and more stable grasps than directly predicting joint targets. Objects are meshes from the datasets, approximated with primitive shapes or decimated for simulation speed. The task is decomposed hierarchically into two phases. In the grasping phase, a low-level policy πg acts on a feature extraction layer φ(s, D) that converts the state (joint angles and velocities, hand-object contact forces, wrist and object 6D poses with velocities) into wrist- and object-relative features, providing invariance to global coordinates; it includes a vertical-distance cue to the supporting surface and goal components G = [g̃x | g̃q | g̃c] encoding 3D distances between current and target joint positions (computed object-relative from the label), angular distances to target joint rotations, and the target binary contact vector. The reward sums joint position error, joint-angle error, a contact reward (fraction of target contacts achieved plus force exerted on desired contact points capped proportionally to object weight), and velocity regularization of hand and object. A wrist-guidance technique biases the translational PD-controller joints with the object-relative target hand position extracted from the label, which the ablations show improves convergence and stability. In the motion synthesis phase, the grasping policy keeps controlling the fingers while a high-level module steers only the wrist: it estimates a 6D target hand pose from the displacement between the object's current and goal poses via closed-loop control, recomputed after every action, so both modules act concurrently to move the object while preserving the grasp.

## Contributions

1. Introduction of the dynamic grasp synthesis task: generating grasping and in-hand object-moving motions from a known object pose, a static grasp reference, and a 6D goal pose.
2. D-Grasp, an RL-based, physics-grounded method with a hierarchical decomposition into a low-level grasping policy and a high-level motion synthesis module, requiring only a single-frame grasp label per sequence.
3. A grasp-label-parameterized reward with explicit contact terms that incentivizes reaching the reference's contact points, producing human-like grasps.
4. Demonstration that the pipeline works with references from three sources — motion capture datasets, static grasp synthesis methods, and image-based pose estimation — and can correct imperfect labels into stable, physically plausible dynamic interactions.

## Experimental Setup

Training uses the authors' PPO implementation with RaiSim; the grasping policy is trained first on all training labels and objects, followed by the motion synthesis component with the pretrained grasping policy. Data comes from DexYCB (1000 grasping sequences, 10 subjects, 20 YCB objects; left-handed sequences filtered out; random 75%/25% train/test split over sequences and subjects) with grasp labels selected as the timestep where object displacement exceeds a threshold; additionally, grasp labels are generated for all DexYCB objects with the grasp synthesis method of Jiang et al. (400/200 train/test labels) and for HO3D objects (200/100 split), and image-based references are obtained on HO3D images using ContactOpt. Metrics: success rate (percentage of sequences maintaining a stable grasp without slipping), interpenetration (penetrating hand volume computed from the MANO mesh against high-resolution object meshes, in cm3), simulated distance (mean object displacement in mm/s), contact ratio (achieved versus target contacts), mean position error (MPE), and geodesic orientation error. Baselines place the label pose directly on the hand and hold it with PD control (*+PD) or after offline inverse-kinematics de-noising (*+IK); a Flat-RL baseline trains the whole task end-to-end without phase separation; Ours+static grasp freezes the policy during motion synthesis; Ours+learned policy replaces the closed-loop motion module with a learned one. Static grasp evaluation is reported on both train and test splits; generalization is averaged over six test sets of three held-out DexYCB objects each; motion synthesis uses a subset of representative YCB objects with 100 randomly sampled out-of-distribution target poses.

## Results

On static grasp evaluation, directly replaying references in simulation fails or penetrates: for DexYCB motion-capture labels, GT+PD reaches 0.31/0.35 success (train/test) with 4.59/4.41 cm3 interpenetration and GT+IK 0.39/0.50 with 9.23/9.74 cm3, whereas D-Grasp achieves 0.70/0.63 success with 1.75/1.77 cm3. With synthetic grasp labels on DexYCB, D-Grasp reaches 0.75/0.73 success versus 0.25/0.24 for Jiang et al. + PD; on HO3D synthetic labels, 0.73/0.71 versus 0.31/0.30. With image-based ContactOpt references on HO3D, D-Grasp reaches 0.88/0.81 success versus 0.67/0.60, and cuts interpenetration from 14.94/14.00 cm3 to 2.67/2.08 cm3; the higher absolute numbers here reflect the already contact-optimized reference quality. Generalization to unseen objects gives 0.56 success versus 0.30 (GT+PD) and 0.38 (GT+IK), with lower simulated distance (9.0 versus 13.7/11.7 mm/s) and interpenetration (1.74 versus 4.41/9.08 cm3). For motion synthesis, the full method reduces mean position error roughly 7-fold relative to Flat-RL (0.08 versus 0.55, in the mm unit reported in the paper's table) and geodesic error from 1.66 to 0.52 rad; Ours+static grasp (0.45 MPE, 1.46 rad) and Ours+learned policy (0.30, 0.92) perform markedly worse, showing that an actively controlled grasp throughout the sequence and the closed-loop motion module are both essential. Ablations on a DexYCB subset (success 0.89 for the full method) show every component matters: removing the contact reward collapses success to 0.0 (contact ratio 0.02), removing the goal space drops it to 0.28, removing the feature extraction layer to 0.47, and removing wrist-guidance to 0.58.

## Limitations

The approach may struggle with thin objects that are difficult to grasp while resting on a surface, and there is a performance drop on unseen test labels and unseen objects (success 0.56 in the generalization study, with room for improvement). Physical parameters such as surface friction, inertia, and center of mass are assumed known a priori, which rarely holds in real-world settings; the authors propose adding a perceptual component to estimate these properties as future work. The method still requires a static hand pose reference for each sequence, including for unseen objects, and interpenetration results are reported on the original high-resolution meshes while training uses simplified collision geometry, which limits achievable interpenetration performance in the original setting.
