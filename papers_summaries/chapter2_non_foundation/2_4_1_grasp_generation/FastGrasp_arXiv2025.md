## Summary

FastGrasp synthesizes diverse, physically plausible grasping poses for a dexterous hand interacting with a given object (given as a point cloud) through a one-stage diffusion framework that removes the contact-map prediction and iterative optimization of prior two-stage methods. The model first trains an autoencoder that maps 778 hand-mesh vertices into a 768-dimensional latent space decoded into 61-dimensional MANO parameters, then learns a latent diffusion model conditioned on PointNet embeddings of the object; because directly injecting physical losses into diffusion training causes oscillations, an MLP Adaptation Module is trained in a second step to align the diffusion output distribution with hand-object physical constraints (consistency, contact, and penetration losses), and DDIM sampling adjusts denoising step size for speed. On the out-of-domain HO-3D benchmark the GRAB-trained model reports penetration volume 5.23 with 0.14 s inference versus ContactGen's 9.96 and 110.60 s; in-domain it reaches penetration volume 4.37 on OakInk and 1.25 on GRAB with 100% contact ratio on GRAB, and a 10-participant user study rates its grasps most natural and stable.

## Background and Problem

Predicting how a human hand grasps an object is difficult because hands have many degrees of freedom with intricate contact patterns, and generated grasps must be both physically plausible and natural. Prior synthesis methods are typically two-stage: a generative model such as a Conditional Variational AutoEncoder (CVAE) produces intermediate representations — contact maps or parts maps based on the interacting objects' point clouds — and a second stage iteratively optimizes hand parameters. This design has two drawbacks: the iterative optimization is computationally intensive, causing low inference efficiency and time-consuming generation, and pose quality relies heavily on the first stage's intermediate representations, making it prone to accumulated errors. Latent diffusion models — following the Stable Diffusion paradigm of training an autoencoder in the data space and then a DDPM on the encoded samples — address the computational intensity of directly training DDPMs on high-resolution point clouds. FastGrasp follows this paradigm and adds an Adaptation Module to inject hand-object physical constraints into the latent representation, supervising the spatial geometry of objects and hand-object interaction in a single stage.

## Method

Latent hand representation: an autoencoder takes hand-mesh vertices h_m of dimension 778x3 as input, processed by a PointNet encoder block; in an asymmetric design, the decoder converts the latent vector into 61-dimensional MANO parameters h_p instead of vertices — fewer degrees of freedom than vertices, improving regularization — with the mesh finally reconstructed from h_p by a differentiable MANO layer. The autoencoder's objective combines reconstruction and physical terms: L_recon = λ1·L_param + λ2·L_mesh, combining MSE on MANO parameters and Chamfer distance on hand vertices, plus three physical losses from prior work: L_consist (predicted hand-mesh contact region consistent with ground truth on the object), L_cmap (the generated hand maintains contact with the object), and L_penets (no penetration of the physical volume); the total is L = L_recon + λ3·L_cmap + λ4·L_penetr + λ5·L_consist. Diffusion model: a denoising U-Net trained on the latent distribution predicts added noise; inputs are the latent z0 (the encoder feature output), an object point cloud o_a as conditioning via a PointNet embedding P, and time step t, with loss L_LDM = E[||ε − ε_θ(z0_t, P(o_a), t)||²_2]. Adaptation Module: directly adding the physical losses during diffusion training causes oscillations, attributed to the difficulty of simultaneously learning the ε output distribution and capturing the physical constraints, with significant penetration and displacement in generated meshes; training is decomposed into two steps. After diffusion training, an MLP-based module f_adapt maps the diffusion output z1 to z2 = f_adapt(z1). To retain the diffusion model's knowledge, z1 is reconstructed through the decoder, h_p = D(z1 + z2) and h_m = f_mano(h_p), while the adaptation module is updated with the same combined loss to align the latent distribution with the physical constraints of hand-object interactions. Inference: noise u sampled from N(0,1) plus the object point cloud enter the diffusion model, which generates the latent prior z1 via N-step denoising; the adaptation module integrates z1 with the object point cloud to generate z2; z1 and z2 are combined, decoded into MANO parameters, and passed through the MANO layer to produce the hand mesh. The generation requires only one pass of network inference, and DDIM optimizes speed and quality by adjusting the step size during denoising.

## Contributions

- FastGrasp, a diffusion-based one-stage model for generating grasping hand pose without requiring expensive iterative optimization.
- An Adaptation Module that effectively incorporates physical constraints into the latent hand-pose representation.
- Fast inference that outperforms previous state-of-the-art methods on a range of metrics.

## Experimental Setup

Three MANO-parameterized hand-object benchmarks are used: OakInk, real human grasps for 1,700 objects from 12 subjects; GRAB, real human grasps for 51 objects across 10 subjects; and HO-3D for out-of-domain evaluation. Following prior protocols, the model is trained separately on OakInk and GRAB, with generalization evaluated on HO-3D and in-domain training and evaluation on OakInk and GRAB. Five metric groups are used: physical plausibility, via hand-object mutual penetration volume (voxelizing the mesh into 1 mm³ cubes and counting overlapping voxels) and contact ratio (proportion of grasps in contact); grasp stability, via simulation displacement — a simulator positions the object and grasps and measures the average displacement of the object's center of gravity due to gravity; diversity, by clustering grasps into 20 clusters with K-means over 3D hand keypoints and reporting the entropy of cluster assignments and the average cluster size; generation speed, the average time per pose over 128 randomly selected objects on an NVIDIA A40 GPU; and a perceptual score from a user study. Implementation: Adam optimizer, LR = 1e-4, latent size N_z = 768, N = 3000, batch size 256. Autoencoder loss weights are λ1 = 0.1, λ2 = 1, λ3 = 1000, λ4 = 10, λ5 = 10; during diffusion training the autoencoder is frozen and 3000 points are sampled from the mesh as the input point cloud; the adaptation module uses the same input cloud with weights λ1_d = 100, λ2_d = 0.1, λ3_d = 1000, λ4_d = 20, λ5_d = 0.1.

## Results

- Out-of-domain HO-3D (Table 2; Ours1 is trained on GRAB, Ours2 and ContactGen2 on OakInk): Ours1 achieves 5.23 penetration volume / 2.14 simulation displacement / 98 contact ratio / 2.88 entropy / 3.97 cluster size / 0.14 s, versus GrabNet 15.50/2.34/99/2.80/2.06/0.23 s, GraspTTA 7.37/5.34/76/2.70/1.43/6.90 s, HALO 25.84/3.02/97/2.81/4.87/10.42 s, GF 93.01/2.70/100/2.75/3.44/32.75 s, ContactGen 9.96/2.70/97/2.81/5.04/110.60 s, and Ours2 12.30/1.44/100/2.88/4.41/0.14 s (ContactGen2 14.32/2.41/100/2.84/5.23/110.60 s). GrabNet matches the generation speed but suffers significant penetration, and ContactGen excels in cluster size but has the longest generation time.
- In-domain (Table 3): on OakInk, Ours 4.37/1.45/94/2.92/4.96 versus GrabNet 6.60/1.21/94/1.68/1.22 and ContactGen* (retrained on OakInk with the authors' released code) 4.85/2.01/94/2.88/4.07; on GRAB, Ours 1.25/1.67/100/2.93/1.87 versus GrabNet 1.72/3.65/96/2.72/1.93, HALO 2.09/3.61/94/2.88/2.15, and ContactGen 2.16/2.72/96/2.88/4.11 — the best value on almost all evaluation metrics, per the paper.
- Ablation (Table 1): the CVAE baseline slightly outperforms the diffusion model in penetration rate but has weaker generative performance (lower entropy and smaller cluster sizes): on OakInk CVAE gives 13.08 penetration volume / 0.78 displacement / 2.81 entropy / 1.12 cluster size versus the original diffusion model's 18.34/1.45/2.91/5.24. Adding a physical loss directly to the diffusion model decreases performance, increasing displacement and reducing robustness (OakInk 6.31/3.77 with contact ratio dropping to 71, GRAB 8.43/5.24 with contact ratio 50). The whole pipeline with the Adaptation Module yields OakInk 4.37/1.45/94/2.92/4.96, GRAB 1.25/1.67/100/2.93/1.87, and HO-3D 5.23/2.14/98/2.88/3.97, notably reducing penetration and improving out-of-domain generalization.
- Supplementary (Table 4, OakInk): without the physical loss (hand parameters input) the model gives 1.53 simulation displacement / 0.93 penetration distance / 4.76 penetration volume / 98 contact ratio; with hand parameters and the physical losses 1.49/0.91/4.61/98; the full vertex-based model 1.83/0.91/2.39/98 — vertices reduce penetration volume relative to MANO parameters (2.39 versus 4.61), attributed to a more robust data representation less sensitive to input variations.
- User study (Section 4.6): grasps from GrabNet, ContactGen, and FastGrasp on 10 objects from GRAB, OakInk, and HO-3D (3 grasps per object per method) were judged by 10 participants selecting the best grasp on naturalness and stability; FastGrasp received the highest number of selections.

## Limitations

The paper contains no dedicated limitations section. Trade-offs stated in the results: the diffusion model alone trails the CVAE baseline in penetration rate while being stronger in diversity; physical losses cannot be integrated directly into diffusion training without oscillations and must be handled by the separate Adaptation Module; in-domain on OakInk the simulation displacement (1.45) is slightly higher than GrabNet's (1.21); and ContactGen reaches larger cluster sizes on GRAB (4.11 versus 1.87) and on the HO-3D comparison (5.04/5.23 versus 3.97/4.41), with FastGrasp instead positioned as superior in penetration and stability.
