## Summary

CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training) is the OpenAI paper that established large-scale contrastive vision-language pre-training as a route to zero-shot visual recognition. It jointly trains an image encoder and a text encoder from scratch to predict which caption goes with which image, on a newly collected dataset of 400 million (image, text) pairs (WIT, WebImageText) gathered from the internet with 500,000 queries. At test time, the text encoder converts the class names of any target dataset into classifier weights, enabling zero-shot transfer: the best model reaches 76.2% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet without using any of its 1.28 million labeled training examples, matching the original supervised ResNet-50, and is benchmarked against over 50 existing systems on more than 30 datasets spanning OCR, geo-localization, action recognition, and fine-grained classification. In the context of this survey, CLIP's shared image-text embedding space is the foundation on which later text-to-3D shape retrieval pipelines for hand-held object reconstruction build: 3D-aware extensions such as OpenShape embed 3D models into this kind of space, allowing a text description of a manipulated object to retrieve or select a matching shape prior.

## Background and Problem

State-of-the-art computer vision systems at the time were trained to predict a fixed set of predetermined categories, which limits generality: any new visual concept requires additional labeled data. The NLP community had instead shifted to task-agnostic pre-training on web-scale raw text, where models such as GPT-3 transfer zero-shot to downstream tasks without dataset-specific heads. Earlier attempts at natural language supervision for vision existed — most notably Visual N-Grams, which achieved only 11.5% zero-shot ImageNet accuracy versus the roughly 88.4% supervised state of the art — and weakly supervised approaches (Instagram hashtag models, JFT-300M) scaled well but hardcoded their output to 1,000 or 18,291 classes with static softmax classifiers, precluding zero-shot flexibility. The paper asks whether scalable pre-training that learns directly from natural language about images can close this gap, and defines zero-shot transfer broadly as generalization to unseen datasets (a proxy for unseen tasks), rather than only to unseen categories within one dataset.

## Method

CLIP pre-trains from scratch on WIT, a dataset of 400 million (image, text) pairs collected from publicly available internet sources by searching with a base list of 500,000 queries (all English Wikipedia words occurring at least 100 times, augmented with high pointwise-mutual-information bigrams and WordNet synsets), class-balanced with up to 20,000 pairs per query. The pre-training objective is efficiency-motivated: a transformer language model trained to generate captions learned ImageNet classes 3x slower than a bag-of-words prediction baseline, and swapping the predictive objective for a contrastive one yielded a further 4x efficiency gain in zero-shot transfer. Given a batch of N (image, text) pairs, CLIP predicts which of the N x N possible pairings occurred by maximizing cosine similarity of the N real pairs and minimizing it for the N^2 - N incorrect ones, with a symmetric cross-entropy loss over temperature-scaled similarity scores (the temperature is a learnable log-parameterized scalar initialized at 0.07 and clipped to keep logits below 100). Image encoders are either a modified ResNet-50 family (ResNet-D improvements, antialiased blur pooling, attention pooling, EfficientNet-style scaling across width, depth, and resolution) or ViT models; the text encoder is a 63M-parameter 12-layer, 512-wide, 8-head byte-pair-encoding Transformer (vocabulary 49,152, sequence length capped at 76) whose [EOS] activation, layer-normalized and linearly projected, is the text feature. Five ResNets (RN50, RN101, RN50x4, RN50x16, RN50x64) and three ViTs (ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16, ViT-L/14) are trained for 32 epochs with Adam with decoupled weight decay, a cosine schedule, batch size 32,768, mixed precision, and a single random square crop as augmentation; the largest ResNet took 18 days on 592 V100 GPUs and the largest ViT 12 days on 256 V100 GPUs. The best model, ViT-L/14@336px, adds one epoch of higher-resolution (336 px) pre-training; zero-shot classification embeds dataset class names through prompt templates ("A photo of a {label}.") and optional ensembles of context prompts (80 prompts on ImageNet), caching the resulting text embeddings as a linear classifier.

## Contributions

- Demonstration that the simple contrastive task of pairing images with their captions is an efficient and scalable way to learn state-of-the-art image representations from scratch on 400 million web-collected (image, text) pairs, with zero-shot transfer performance following a smooth log-log scaling law across a 44x compute range.
- Zero-shot classification via natural language: the text encoder acts as a hypernetwork that synthesizes classifier weights from class names and prompts, removing the need for dataset-specific training heads, with documented prompt-engineering and embedding-space ensembling recipes.
- State-of-the-art linear-probe representation quality: the best CLIP model outperforms the best previously available models (e.g., Noisy Student EfficientNet-L2) in compute efficiency, with ViT encoders about 3x more compute-efficient than the ResNet variants.
- Evidence that zero-shot CLIP models have much higher effective robustness to natural distribution shift than supervised ImageNet models of equal in-distribution accuracy, shrinking the robustness gap by up to 75%.
- A systematic analysis of zero-shot behavior (data efficiency, comparison to few-shot learning and humans, data overlap between pre-training and evaluation sets) plus an early examination of social bias, class-design sensitivity, and surveillance capability for general-purpose vision models.

## Experimental Setup

Evaluation spans over 30 existing datasets covering object classification (ImageNet, CIFAR10/100, STL10, PascalVOC2007, SUN397), fine-grained classification (Stanford Cars, Food101, Flowers102, FGVCAircraft, OxfordPets, Birdsnap), OCR and scene text (Rendered SST2, HatefulMemes, MNIST), action recognition in videos (Kinetics700, UCF101), geo-localization (Country211), traffic signs (GTSRB), and specialized tasks (EuroSAT, RESISC45, PatchCamelyon, CLEVRCounts, KITTI Distance), with comparisons to over 50 existing systems including EfficientNet-NoisyStudent, BiT-M/S, SimCLRv2, BYOL, MoCo, Instagram-pretrained ResNeXt, and ViT (ImageNet-21k). Zero-shot CLIP is compared against fully supervised logistic regression on ResNet-50 features, few-shot (1- to 128-shot and full) linear probes on the same CLIP feature space, robustness benchmarks (ImageNetV2, ImageNet Sketch, Youtube-BB/ImageNet-Vid, ObjectNet, ImageNet-A, ImageNet-R), human subjects on Oxford-IIT Pets (3,669 test images, five raters, zero-/one-/two-shot conditions), a duplicate-detection data-overlap analysis across 35 datasets, and bias probes on FairFace and images of Members of Congress.

## Results

Zero-shot CLIP reaches 76.2% top-1 (95% top-5, matching Inception-v4) on ImageNet, versus 11.5% for Visual N-Grams, and improves on Visual N-Grams from 72.4 to 98.4 on aYahoo (a 95% error reduction) and from 23.0 to 58.5 on SUN. Across a 27-dataset suite, zero-shot CLIP beats a fully supervised linear probe on ResNet-50 features on 16 of 27 datasets, including ImageNet, and achieves 99.3% on STL10; it outperforms a ResNet-50 on action recognition by 14.5% on Kinetics700 and 7.7% on UCF101, but underperforms on specialized or abstract tasks (EuroSAT, RESISC45, PatchCamelyon, CLEVRCounts, GTSRB, KITTI Distance). Zero-shot CLIP matches a 4-shot logistic regression on the same feature space and roughly matches the best public 16-shot classifier (BiT-M ResNet152x2); estimated data efficiency of zero-shot transfer has a median of 5.4 labeled examples per class (mean 20.8, range from below 1 to 184). Zero-shot performance remains 10-25% below fully supervised linear probes on most datasets (correlation r = 0.82), approaching supervision only on STL10, CIFAR10, Food101, OxfordPets, and Caltech101. In linear-probe evaluation, the largest ResNet slightly outperforms Noisy Student EfficientNet-L2 on the 12-dataset Kornblith suite, ViT encoders are about 3x more compute-efficient, and ViT-L/14@336px outperforms the best prior model by 2.6% on that suite and by about 5% on the broader 27-dataset suite, winning 21 of 27 per-dataset comparisons; the largest gains come on OCR (SST2, HatefulMemes), geo-localization and scene recognition (Country211, SUN397), and video action recognition (Kinetics700, UCF101), with GTSRB improved by 14.7%. On 7 natural distribution shifts, zero-shot CLIP cuts the gap between ImageNet and shift accuracy by up to 75% — for a banana-class example, ViT-L/14@336px versus an accuracy-matched ResNet-101 gains +5.8% on ImageNetV2, +74.4% on ImageNet-R, +51.2% on ImageNet-A, +39.7% on ObjectNet, and +35.0% on ImageNet Sketch — while supervised adaptation to ImageNet (+9.2% to 85.4%) yields no robustness improvement and even loses 4.7% on ImageNet-R. Detected pre-training/evaluation data overlap has a median of 2.2% (mean 3.2%) across 35 datasets and inflates overall accuracy by at most 0.6% (Birdsnap). Humans reach only 54% zero-shot on Oxford-IIT Pets (majority vote 57%) versus CLIP's 93.5%, but jump to 76% with one example per class, exposing the remaining gap between machine and human few-sample learning.

## Limitations

The paper's own limitations section notes that zero-shot CLIP is, on average, only competitive with a linear probe on ResNet-50 features — well below overall state of the art — and that an estimated 1000x more compute would be needed for zero-shot CLIP to reach overall state-of-the-art performance, which the authors call infeasible with current hardware, motivating research into computational and data efficiency. Zero-shot performance is weak on fine-grained differentiation (car models, flower species, aircraft variants), abstract and systematic tasks such as counting, and near-random on truly novel tasks such as estimating distance to the nearest car. Generalization to genuinely out-of-distribution data remains brittle: CLIP attains only 88% on handwritten MNIST digits, below a logistic regression on raw pixels, because few digit-like images exist in the pre-training data. CLIP can only choose among the concepts specified in a zero-shot classifier and cannot generate novel outputs like a captioning model; joint contrastive-generative training is proposed as future work. Data efficiency is poor — iterating the 12.8 billion images seen over 32 epochs at one image per second would take 405 years. Methodologically, validation sets were repeatedly queried during development, weakening the strict zero-shot protocol, and the 27-dataset evaluation suite is acknowledged as co-adapted with CLIP's capabilities. Trained on unfiltered internet pairs, CLIP learns social biases: in denigration probes, 4.9% of FairFace images were misclassified into non-human classes, rising to about 14% for images of Black individuals (all other races under 8%), and 16.5% of male images versus 9.8% of female images were classified into crime-related categories; class design and probability thresholds substantially alter outputs. In surveillance-oriented tests, CLIP scored 91.8% on coarse CCTV scene classification but dropped to 51.1% under close-distractor stress tests, performed near-randomly on fine-grained detection, and achieved 59.2% zero-shot top-1 celebrity identification with 100 classes (43.3% with 1,000), which the authors flag for its societal implications.
