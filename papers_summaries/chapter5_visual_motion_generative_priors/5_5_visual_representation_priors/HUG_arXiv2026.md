## Summary

HUG is, to the authors' knowledge, the first dexterous grasping framework trained purely on human data and deployable across multiple robot embodiments. It rests on three artifacts. 1M-HUGS is an egocentric dataset of one million RGB frames (plus one million grayscale frames, two million training entries in total, 27.8 hours) of natural human grasps covering 6,707 recordings, roughly 1.5K unique objects, and 41 buildings, captured with Aria Gen 2 smart glasses. HUG itself is a point-conditioned flow-matching model that fuses RGB and depth observations into a 99-dimensional MANO grasp, parameterized by wrist translation, 6D wrist rotation, and 15 finger joint rotations, for any user-clicked object in a single RGB-D image. HUG-BENCH is a benchmark of 90 unseen objects in five geometric categories and three size bins with metric-scale meshes, evaluated both in MuJoCo and in the real world. On the 30-object real test set HUG reaches 66.7 percent tabletop success, beating Dex1B by 23 points and CAP by 34 points, and 62.0 percent in-the-wild with zero-shot transfer across cameras, robot hands, and unseen households.

## Background and Problem

Multi-fingered robot grasping of arbitrary objects remains far from human generality, and the bottleneck is data: robots lack the diverse real-world grasping experience humans accumulate daily. Synthetic grasp generation (force-closure optimization, learned generators, reinforcement learning) suffers the sim-to-real gap and typically requires retraining per robot hand; teleoperation yields real grasps but is tedious and cannot cover the open world; web-image-based human datasets scale but lack precise 3D signals. The paper argues that natural human grasps, collected in the wild, are the right training distribution because they model how people actually grasp rather than every physically valid grasp. Two recent advances make this practical: lightweight egocentric sensors such as Aria Gen 2 stream calibrated RGB-D and hand tracking, making collection as easy as wearing glasses, and anthropomorphic robot hands with learned retargeting have narrowed the human-robot morphology gap, enabling a collect-learn-retarget pipeline with no robot data at all.

## Method

Data collection exploits a simple protocol: a wearer moves their head for 15 to 30 seconds around a static object, then grasps it with the right hand; the grasp pose is propagated back into the preceding no-hand frames using the glasses' camera poses, so one physical grasp yields hundreds of (object-only image, grasp) pairs from diverse viewpoints at no annotation cost. Curation uses a vision-language model to identify the grasped object (about 0.003 dollars per recording), SAM3 for bidirectional mask propagation, stability and proximity heuristics for grasp-frame selection, and human review of every recording; frame filters require a non-empty mask, at least 60 percent confident stereo depth from S2M2, hand-landmark-to-mask intersection, at least five landmarks in bounds, and no hand in frame. Aria's sparse 21-landmark skeleton is fitted with a full articulated MANO hand via per-frame anatomically constrained L-BFGS optimization (average fingertip error below 2 mm), with the MANO shape fixed at one canonical value so the same pose denotes the same grasp across collectors. The model takes an RGB-D image and a 2D click lifted to a 3D query point. A frozen DINOv2-Base ViT with register tokens produces 256 patch tokens; the metric point cloud is cropped to a 0.3 m radius ball around the query point, and 4,096 sampled points pass through a trainable PointNeXt U-Net producing 256 region tokens. Point-painting fusion projects each point cloud centroid onto the image, bilinearly samples the DINOv2 patch feature, concatenates it with the point token, and projects to 1024 dimensions, after which a 4-layer transformer cross-attends the fused tokens to the Fourier-encoded query token. The flow transformer splits the grasp into three normalized group tokens (translation, wrist rotation, fingers), processed by 6 DiT blocks with AdaLN-Zero timestep conditioning and cross-attention to the scene tokens, and the velocity field is integrated with 50-step Euler sampling. Training combines velocity MSE with a (1-t)-weighted L1 loss on MANO 3D hand landmarks (weights 1 and 20), runs 100K AdamW steps at batch size 128 with an EMA from step 50K, and takes about 10 hours on two RTX 5090 GPUs; the frozen DINOv2 accounts for 86.6M of 207.2M total parameters, with 120.7M trainable. Camera intrinsics enter only through geometric back-projection and projection, never as learned parameters, which is what allows transfer across stereo cameras.

## Contributions

The paper claims four open-sourced contributions. First, the framework itself, the first grasping system trained purely on human data and deployable across multiple robot embodiments without per-hand training. Second, 1M-HUGS, whose in-the-wild human grasps with MANO-fit poses and metric depth contrast with simulation-only datasets like Dex1B or lab-collected ones like DexYCB, and whose back-propagated grasps turn one physical grasp into hundreds of training pairs. Third, the HUG model, a point-conditioned flow-matching architecture whose dual-modal RGB-plus-point-cloud input, targeted 0.3 m crop, point painting, and auxiliary 3D landmark loss each prove individually important in ablations. Fourth, HUG-BENCH, which starts from real objects, reconstructs them into metric-scale simulation assets from five egocentric views using a modified Multi-view SAM3D pipeline with Alpha Wrap watertighting and CoACD convex decomposition, records ten human grasps per object as an oracle, and pairs simulation with real-robot evaluation on 30 purchasable test objects.

## Experimental Setup

Simulation evaluation in MuJoCo uses a position-actuated fixed-shape MANO right hand executing an open-loop pre-grasp, grasp, and 0.5 m lift rollout, with success defined as the object leaving the surface; success rate and fingertip contact error are averaged over 10 grasps per object on 60 validation and 30 test objects, and checkpoints are selected by best validation success rate. Real-world tabletop experiments deploy HUG on a 6-DoF Ability hand mounted on a 7-DoF xArm with a third-party ZED stereo camera, 10 trials per object for 300 trials per method, against Dex1B (generative grasping trained on 1B simulated demonstrations) and CAP (contact-anchored parallel-jaw policies); severe table-intersecting grasps count as failures. In-the-wild experiments use a modified YOR mobile manipulator with an AgileX NERO arm, a 20-DoF WUJI hand, and Aria Gen 2 vision, running all 300 trials consecutively in one morning across rooms of an unseen household with no onsite tuning. Object placement difficulty is quantified by displacing ground-truth grasps along all three wrist axes before execution.

## Results

In simulation the full model reaches 71.5 percent validation and 73.0 percent test success rate with 14.6 mm fingertip contact error on test objects, about 20 points below the recorded human-grasp oracle (90.3 and 94.0 percent, 7.4 mm). Ablations show the auxiliary 3D loss is the most critical component: removing it cuts test success to 32.7 percent and more than doubles contact error to 35.7 mm; removing the 0.3 m crop or point painting each costs roughly 10 validation and 15 test points. Single-modality tests reveal complementarity: point-cloud-only prediction remains strong (70.7 percent test success) but lacks semantic grounding, while RGB-only collapses to 29.7 percent with 108.6 mm contact error. Data scaling from 25K to 1M frames lifts test success from 33 to 73 percent and reduces contact error from 54.2 to 14.6 mm without saturating, indicating the model remains data-bound rather than capacity-bound. In the real world, HUG achieves 66.7 percent tabletop success versus 43.7 percent for Dex1B and 32.7 percent for CAP, grasping 28 of 30 objects at least once; it is alone in succeeding on large prismatic objects (storage bin 10 of 10 versus 0 for both baselines) and far ahead on handle-bearing or irregular objects (picnic basket 9 of 10 versus 1 and 0). In-the-wild success is 62.0 percent, only 4.7 points below tabletop, with 29 of 30 objects grasped at least once. A precision analysis shows that displacing ground-truth grasps by 2 cm per axis roughly halves success for medium and large objects and cuts small objects below 20 percent, confirming the benchmark rewards precise placement. Failure analysis attributes most real-world failures to the closing stage (hitting the object in 42 and 57 of 300 trials, tabletop and wild respectively) or to slips and drops during lift, pointing to motion planning and force-aware closing as remedies.

## Limitations

The paper's dedicated limitations section reports that the model handles right-hand grasps only, with the MANO shape fixed at a canonical value, so left-handed or bimanual grasping and hand-size-specific morphology are not modeled, and some object orientations are easier to grasp than others. Human-to-robot retargeting can fail when the robot hand cannot reach a feasible analog of the predicted MANO pose. Real-world rollouts run open-loop with no closed-loop visual feedback during contact or lift, causing failures on objects that shift or articulate. Grasp labels are noisy when the hand is occluded, because Aria Gen 2 tracking degrades and the recorded grasp can be too loose or too tight. Accuracy drops on very small objects, limited by the 224 by 224 input resolution, and on large or distant objects that are rare in egocentric data. The system predicts and executes a single grasp per trial rather than generating and selecting among candidates, and the evaluation is restricted to indoor scenes.
