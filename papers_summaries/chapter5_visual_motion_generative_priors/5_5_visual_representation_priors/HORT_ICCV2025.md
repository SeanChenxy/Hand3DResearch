# HORT: Monocular Hand-held Objects Reconstruction with Transformers

**Authors:** Zerui Chen, Rolandos Alexandros Potamias, Shizhe Chen, Cordelia Schmid  
**Date:** 2025-10-19  
**Identifier:** DOI `10.1109/ICCV51701.2025.00571`  
**Zotero item:** `57XDY5DZ` ([Zotero](zotero://select/library/items/57XDY5DZ))

## Summary

HORT is a coarse-to-fine, fully feed-forward transformer framework that reconstructs dense 3D point clouds (16,384 points) of hand-held objects from a single monocular RGB image, published at ICCV 2025. It couples DINOv2 image features with fine-grained 3D hand geometry from a MANO hand estimate to jointly predict the object's translation relative to the hand palm and a sparse 2,048-point cloud, which a dense decoder then refines using pixel-aligned image features. Across four benchmarks (ObMan, HO3D, DexYCB, MOW) it surpasses implicit-function and diffusion-based state-of-the-art methods, for example reaching FS@10 of 0.88 on ObMan with predicted hands, while running at 16.9 frames per second including hand prediction and requiring only 258 GFLOPs, roughly two orders of magnitude faster than implicit-function competitors and about 200 times faster than the diffusion-based D-SCO.

## Background and Problem

Reconstructing hand-held objects in 3D from monocular images matters for action recognition, human-computer interaction, and robotic manipulation, but object diversity, occlusion, and monocular ambiguity make it substantially harder than hand reconstruction. Optimization-based methods achieve accurate poses or shapes only with extra 3D inputs (multi-view images, video, depth, or known CAD models) and hours of iterative optimization, making them impractical in real time. Learning-based methods predominantly use deep implicit functions such as signed distance fields or directed distance fields; these produce overly smooth surfaces that miss fine geometry, and extracting explicit meshes requires a slow Marching Cubes post-process. The prior state of the art D-SCO reconstructs explicit point clouds with diffusion models, but multi-step denoising costs more than 13 seconds per reconstruction. The earlier vertex-based HO method of Hasson et al. is fast but limited in resolution. HORT therefore targets the balance between reconstruction accuracy and inference speed for explicit 3D prediction from a single image.

## Method

The model has four modules: an image encoder, a hand encoder, a sparse point cloud decoder, and a dense point cloud decoder. A state-of-the-art hand pose estimation model provides the MANO hand mesh (778 vertices, 16 rigged joints) and camera parameters; the hand encoder expresses every vertex in 22 local coordinate systems (16 joints, 5 fingertips, and 1 palm) and concatenates an absolute vertex index, yielding a 778 by 67 representation that a PointNet with five MLP layers maps to a 1024-dimensional hand feature. The image encoder is DINOv2-Large (24 transformer layers) applied to 224 by 224 inputs with patch size 14, producing 257 tokens of dimension 1024; earlier layers are frozen and only the last twelve are fine-tuned, balancing pre-trained knowledge against hand-object domain adaptation. Reconstruction happens in the hand palm coordinate system and is disentangled into canonical shape prediction and translation-only pose estimation, since rotation prediction is ill-posed for symmetric everyday objects. The sparse decoder shares a single 10-layer transformer (8 heads, 512-dimensional point tokens) over 1 plus 2,048 learnable tokens that self-attend and cross-attend to image and hand features, the first token regressing the hand-relative 3D translation and the rest decoding the rotated sparse cloud. The dense decoder removes the CLS token, reshapes the remaining patch tokens into a 16 by 16 by 128 feature map via 3 by 3 convolutions, projects the sparse points into the camera frame using the estimated palm location and object translation, and retrieves pixel-aligned features by bilinear interpolation; two blocks of kNN self-attention (k=16) over object points and hand vertices, each with factor-2 and factor-4 upsampling and 3D offset regression, grow the cloud to 16,384 points. Training is end-to-end with an l1 translation loss and Chamfer distance losses on both sparse and dense clouds, weighted by two loss-balancing hyperparameters set to two.

## Contributions

First, a coarse-to-fine transformer framework that efficiently reconstructs dense hand-held object point clouds from monocular images, explicitly balancing quality against compute in contrast to slow implicit fields and diffusion pipelines. Second, the integration of fine-grained 3D hand geometry, encoded through multiple joint coordinate systems, with image features for joint pose and point cloud prediction, exploiting the implicit geometric cues that hand shape and pose provide about the manipulated object, together with pixel-aligned features for dense upsampling. Third, state-of-the-art accuracy across four diverse benchmarks with strong generalization to in-the-wild images at a runtime of 16.9 frames per second including hand prediction, enabled by end-to-end training that previous pipelines such as D-SCO did not use.

## Experimental Setup

Training and evaluation use ObMan, a synthetic dataset of 2,772 ShapeNet objects with 21K GraspIt! grasps (141K train, 6,285 test, with a random 30K subset for ablations), HO3Dv3 (68 sequences, ten subjects, ten YCB objects, 77K train and 1,221 test samples), DexYCB (right-hand s0 split, 29K train and 5,928 test), and MOW (350 train and 92 test YouTube images). Metrics are Chamfer distance in cm^2 over 30K sampled points, F-score at 5 mm and 10 mm thresholds, contact ratio, and penetration depth. Models train with batch size 192 for 50 epochs, Adam at base learning rate 1e-4 with cosine decay, random rotation between -90 and 90 degrees, resizing, bounding-box shifting, and color jittering; ground-truth camera parameters and hand poses are used in training but not assumed at test time, where an off-the-shelf hand model supplies them. The ObMan model takes about 50 hours on four NVIDIA A100 GPUs, and ObMan-pretrained weights are fine-tuned for the real benchmarks; a second MOW setting trains jointly on DexYCB, HOI4D, and MOW. Runtime is measured on an A100 including hand prediction.

## Results

On ObMan with predicted hands HORT reaches FS@5 of 0.66, FS@10 of 0.88, and Chamfer distance of 1.0, against 0.61, 0.81, and 1.1 for D-SCO and 0.44 FS@5 for gSDF; with ground-truth hands it improves to 0.72, 0.91, and 0.9. On HO3D it attains 0.45 FS@5, 0.72 FS@10, and 2.6 CD versus 0.41, 0.63, and 3.4 for D-SCO, a relative improvement of 14.3 percent on FS@10 and 23.5 percent on CD. On DexYCB it reports 0.63 FS@5, 0.85 FS@10, and 1.1 CD, improving FS@10 and CD over D-SCO and clearly beating the video-based gSDF (0.44, 0.71, 3.4). On MOW it matches or exceeds D-SCO under the ObMan-fine-tuned protocol (0.30, 0.49, 10.2 versus 0.30, 0.47, 13.5) and reaches 0.37, 0.57, and 5.4 with multi-dataset training, far above MCC-HO (0.15, 0.31, 15.2) and WildHOI (0.08, 0.20, 27.2). The runtime comparison is decisive: HORT needs 258 GFLOPs and 366M parameters for 16.9 fps, whereas implicit methods exceed 6,000 GFLOPs at around 0.4 fps and D-SCO runs at 0.08 fps; attaching an off-the-shelf meshing step keeps DexYCB FS@10 at 0.85 while reducing speed to 4.8 fps. Ablations show the hand encoder raises FS@5 from 0.45 to 0.53 with palm-frame vertices and to 0.60 with the 22 joint coordinate systems (CD from 3.1 to 1.8); fine-tuned DINOv2 beats training a ViT from scratch (0.51) and a frozen DINOv2 (0.48); joint decoding of pose and points beats separate heads; and pixel-aligned features plus hand features in the dense decoder lift FS@5 from 0.54 to 0.58 and then 0.60.

## Limitations

Accuracy depends on an off-the-shelf hand pose estimation model at test time, whose predictions and runtime are part of the reported pipeline. Object pose is restricted to a translation relative to the hand palm because rotation estimation is ill-posed for symmetric objects, so full 6D object orientation is not predicted. The output is a point cloud, and while mesh fitting is straightforward, it costs speed (16.9 to 4.8 fps with meshing). Benchmark comparisons rely on per-dataset fine-tuning from the ObMan-pretrained model, and absolute quality drops notably on real and in-the-wild data, for example MOW FS@5 of 0.30 to 0.37 versus 0.66 on synthetic ObMan; the paper states that robustness to occlusion and failure cases are analyzed in supplementary material.
