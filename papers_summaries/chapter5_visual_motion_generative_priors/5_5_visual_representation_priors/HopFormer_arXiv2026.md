# HopFormer: Sparse Graph Transformers with Explicit Receptive Field Control

**Authors:** Sanggeon Yun, Raheeb Hassan, Ryozo Masukawa, Sungheon Jeong, Mohsen Imani  
**Date:** 2026-02-02  
**Identifier:** [arXiv:2602.02268](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02268)

## Summary

HopFormer is a graph Transformer that injects graph structure exclusively through head-specific n-hop masked sparse attention, dispensing with both explicit positional or structural encodings and any architectural modification of the vanilla Transformer. Because the n-hop masks are applied before attention score computation, the model performs genuinely sparse attention whose cost scales linearly with mask sparsity, and each attention head exposes an explicit, interpretable trade-off between receptive field size and compute. Under the standardized OpenGT protocols, this minimal design is competitive with or superior to encoding-heavy graph Transformers, ranking best on six of nine node-classification benchmarks (for example 0.8169 accuracy on Wisconsin and 0.7997 ROC-AUC on OGBG-MolHIV) and best on Peptides-Struct among graph-level tasks, while remaining the only top-performing method whose results are stable across datasets with different small-world characteristics.

## Background and Problem

Graph Transformers were introduced to overcome the strictly local propagation of message-passing GNNs, which causes over-smoothing and information bottlenecks in deep architectures, but existing designs consistently bolt structural information onto the Transformer in one of two ways: positional or structural encodings (shortest-path distances, centrality, Laplacian eigenvectors, diffusion kernels, random-walk statistics) added to attention logits or node embeddings, or architectural modifications such as hybrid message-passing-plus-attention blocks and auxiliary structural tokens. The paper argues that both habits deserve scrutiny. Encodings are sensitive to their formulation, dimensionality, scaling, and integration point, and they can impair generalization when test-time graph sizes or structural distributions differ from training. Meanwhile, dense global self-attention costs O(|V|^2) per layer, and when nodes and edges are both used as tokens (a common strategy for heterogeneous graph information) the attention matrix scales as (|V|+|E|) x (|V|+|E|), which can reach O(|V|^4) in dense graphs. Since many real-world graphs exhibit small-world structure, with a high clustering coefficient and average shortest-path length of order O(log |V|), informative interactions concentrate within a few hops, raising the question of whether dense global attention is necessary at all. The paper also draws on sequence-modeling results showing that causal masking alone lets Transformers recover positional information without positional embeddings, and asks whether graph structure can likewise be injected purely through constrained attention patterns.

## Method

HopFormer keeps the standard Transformer encoder intact (residual connections, normalization, feed-forward blocks) and changes only the attention support. First, an edge-to-node augmentation converts the input graph G=(V,E) into an augmented incidence graph in which every edge becomes an edge-node linked to its endpoints, giving N+M tokens with Theta(M) incidence links and preserving sparsity up to a constant factor. Node and edge attributes are embedded by lightweight modality-specific linear projectors into a shared d-dimensional token space; if edge features are absent they are set to zero so edge tokens contribute only through topology. Second, each attention head h receives a hop budget n_h and a mask defined as the n_h-hop reachability matrix of the augmented graph, computed from powers of its adjacency matrix. Attention is computed only over token pairs within the mask support, both for scores and for softmax normalization, so the operation is true sparse attention with cost proportional to nnz(M) rather than masked dense attention; per-layer complexity is flexible, scaling at least linearly in |V|+|E|. For node-level tasks a shared classifier reads node tokens, and for graph-level tasks a permutation-invariant sum or mean readout pools node and edge tokens. The paper proves two results under standard assumptions (full-rank value projections, injective post-attention mapping, sparse topology with bounded hops, and at least two distinct hop budgets): Theorem 1 shows the output of a head with budget n_h depends exclusively on the n_h-hop neighborhood, so masks alone make the layer graph-aware, and Theorem 2 shows a layer whose heads have distinct hop budgets is strictly more expressive than any layer with a shared budget, enabling multi-scale representation without extra depth.

## Contributions

The paper demonstrates, theoretically and empirically, that explicit positional or structural encodings and architectural modifications are not prerequisites for effective graph Transformers: head-specific n-hop masked sparse attention alone suffices to inject graph structure, with a sufficiency corollary and an expressiveness theorem backing the claim. Second, it provides explicit and interpretable receptive field control, where each head trades receptive field size against computation through its hop budget, in contrast to prior sparse or distance-restricted Transformers whose sparsity is tied to fixed encodings or applied as secondary constraints. Third, through a systematic small-world analysis of the benchmarks it shows that dense global attention offers diminishing returns: on node-level datasets with strong small-world structure, localized receptive fields yield more stable and consistently high performance, while on graph-level datasets with weaker small-world effects the top Transformer variants converge to similar ranks. A comparison table positions HopFormer as the only architecture that preserves the vanilla Transformer (no encodings, no modifications) while achieving sparsity guided by n-hop masks.

## Experimental Setup

All experiments follow the standardized, fully reproducible OpenGT benchmark protocols, with every baseline run inside the OpenGT codebase under identical hyperparameter search spaces and training protocols. Baselines include the graph Transformers DIFFormer, NodeFormer, GraphGPS, Graphormer, GRIT, SGFormer, SAN, SpecFormer, Exphormer, GraphTransformer, GraphMLPMixer, CoBFormer, and DeGTA, plus the message-passing GCN, GAT, and APPNP. Node-level benchmarks span citation networks (Cora, Citeseer, Pubmed), web graphs (Chameleon, Squirrel), an actor co-occurrence graph (Actor), and heterophilous university networks (Texas, Cornell, Wisconsin); graph-level benchmarks cover molecular property prediction (ZINC, OGBG-MolHIV, OGBG-MolPCBA) and the LRGB peptide tasks (Peptides-Func, Peptides-Struct). HopFormer fixes four attention heads and searches per-head hops over {1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48}; all results are mean and standard deviation over 3 runs on a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 with 24 GB memory, using best-performing configurations within the unified search space. Small-world characteristics (dataset-level clustering coefficient and average shortest-path length) are measured for every benchmark to contextualize method behavior.

## Results

On node classification HopFormer ranks best on Citeseer (0.6850), Chameleon (0.5738), Squirrel (0.3748), Cornell (0.7657), Texas (0.7937), and Wisconsin (0.8169), and second on Cora (0.7850 versus 0.7913 for GraphTransformer), with 0.7630 on Pubmed, while several encoding-heavy baselines (Graphormer, SAN, GRIT, DeGTA) report out-of-memory on Pubmed. On graph-level tasks it achieves the best OGBG-MolHIV ROC-AUC of 0.7997 and the best Peptides-Struct MAE of 0.2503 (versus 0.2512 for GPS), with 0.1849 AP on OGBG-MolPCBA, 0.6467 AP on Peptides-Func, and 0.1306 MAE on ZINC. Rank-trajectory analysis shows that competing methods fluctuate strongly as dataset structure changes, whereas HopFormer holds consistently strong ranks; among methods that achieve rank 1 on at least one benchmark, only HopFormer combines high median performance with narrow variance across low, mid, and high terciles of both small-world measures. On graph-level benchmarks with weaker small-world effects, top methods converge to similar ranks, indicating global attention saturates. Finally, forward-pass FLOPs measured on the heterophilous university datasets follow a clear linear relationship with the number of nonzero entries in the n-hop masks, confirming the complexity analysis.

## Limitations

The paper's own limitations discussion states that the link between small-world properties and optimal receptive fields remains primarily empirical, lacking a theoretical characterization of when and why particular hop budgets suffice. Consequently, hop configurations must still be tuned: the per-head n-hop values are searched over a discrete grid, and automatic selection or learning of these configurations is left as future work to reduce reliance on hyperparameter tuning and improve robustness across new domains and scales. The theoretical guarantees additionally rely on bounded hop budgets (a small constant independent of graph size) and, in the experiments, a fixed head count of four, so behavior at much larger hop budgets or head counts is not reported in the paper. It should also be noted that the paper targets graph-structured data in general rather than hand-object interaction or 3D vision tasks specifically.
